    {\fontsize{14}{17}\selectfont \textbf{ABSTRACT}}\\[16pt]
\end{center}

Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) deployed across Internet of Things (IoT) ecosystems face severe operational hurdles arising from decentralized edge topologies, acute class imbalance, and pervasive evaluation data leakage. Although Federated Graph Neural Networks (FedGNNs) offer a compelling framework for relational anomaly modeling without centralizing sensitive NetFlow records, standard architectures frequently suffer from catastrophic false-negative collapse on rare intrusion vectors under empirical risk minimization. To address these challenges, we develop a contrastive hybrid federated GNN architecture and benchmark it using an audited, signature-grouped evaluation protocol on the official NF-ToN-IoT dataset that guarantees zero flow-signature leakage. The system integrates three domain-specialized Graph Attention Network (GAT) client encoders, Leiden-based community partitioning, centroid pooling, an aggregated GraphSAGE server overlay, and downstream Random Forest feature fusion. To overcome severe minority class degradation, we formulate a joint optimization objective uniting Class-Balanced Focal (CB-Focal) loss ($\beta=0.9999, \gamma=2.0$) with Supervised Contrastive (SupCon) representation regularization ($\mu = 0.1$), supported by dynamic class-balanced sampling ($M=64$) and intra-class edge interpolation. Across three independent random seeds on 1,377,837 NetFlow records, our proposed framework reduces Macro False Negative Rate (FNR) from $21.89 \pm 2.01\%$ to $5.72 \pm 1.20\%$, elevating Balanced Accuracy from $78.11 \pm 2.01\%$ to $94.28 \pm 1.20\%$. Crucially, contrastive metric regularization resolves a catastrophic topological collapse wherein $99.83\%$ of rare Backdoor attacks were misclassified as Injection flows by baseline GNNs, rescuing Backdoor detection recall from $0.11 \pm 0.08\%$ to $99.57 \pm 0.16\%$.

\vspace{12pt}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Federated Learning, Graph Attention Networks, IoT Intrusion Detection, Supervised Contrastive Learning, Class Imbalance, Data Leakage, Leiden Community Detection.

\section{Introduction and Background}\label{sec:background}

The exponential proliferation of Internet of Things (IoT) technologies and cyber-physical systems has radically transformed global computing infrastructure across critical sectors including industrial supervisory control, smart electrical power distribution grids, intelligent healthcare patient telemetry, urban municipal transport management, and connected defense installations. Contemporary operational networks no longer consist of homogenous enterprise workstations situated behind well-defined physical network perimeters. Rather, operational networks now span diverse, geographically distributed edge nodes, encompassing low-power field microcontrollers, environmental sensors, programmable logic controllers (PLCs), cellular gateways, and robotic actuators. Although this decentralized infrastructure enables real-time sensing, localized automation, and broad operational visibility, it dramatically widens network perimeter exposure and introduces numerous unmonitored entry vectors.

In contrast to enterprise servers that benefit from dedicated cryptographic co-processors, high-throughput compute clusters, and proactive administrative patching, edge IoT endpoints function under tight hardware constraints. Microcontrollers and embedded gateways typically operate with restricted processing budgets, kilobyte- or megabyte-scale memory, battery limitations, and unreliable wireless links. Compounding these constraints, long operational lifecycles and fragmented vendor ecosystems make systematic firmware updates rare in field deployments. As a result, endpoints frequently run deprecated cryptographic routines, rely on default or static credentials, and harbor unmitigated firmware vulnerabilities, rendering them susceptible to automated exploitation.

The threat landscape confronting IoT networks is marked by an alarming evolution in adversarial sophistication. Adversaries increasingly launch structured, multi-phase intrusions tailored to distributed topologies. High-profile botnet strains, including Mirai and Mozi derivatives, routinely sweep subnets using automated port probes and credential dictionary attacks, aggregating vulnerable endpoints into distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) botnets capable of saturating core infrastructure with terabit-scale floods. In parallel, advanced persistent threat (APT) actors establish low-frequency backdoor command-and-control (C2) sessions, using credential harvesting and living-off-the-land techniques to move laterally within operational technology (OT) subnets without triggering volumetric alarms.

To safeguard connected networks against these persistent threats, Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) serve as an indispensable defensive layer. Traditional NIDS architectures rely predominantly on centralized data ingestion models. In these legacy frameworks, distributed edge gateways and packet sensors are tasked with continuously capturing raw packet payloads (PCAP) or aggregate NetFlow/IPFIX flow records and forwarding this voluminous telemetry across wide-area networks to a centralized Security Operations Center (SOC) or cloud-hosted analytics platform. Once aggregated, central analytics engines apply machine learning and deep neural models in batch pipelines to flag anomalous flow signatures.

Nevertheless, relying on centralized telemetry collection introduces fundamental operational and architectural roadblocks in distributed IoT environments:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Prohibitive Bandwidth and Energy Overhead}: Continuously streaming raw NetFlow logs or packet traces across wide-area links saturates constrained cellular or LPWAN backhauls and rapidly drains the battery life of remote sensor nodes.
    \item \textbf{Detection Latency Bottlenecks}: Centralized batch ingestion introduces processing latencies that prevent timely detection and automated mitigation of volumetric surges or rapid worm propagation in safety-critical industrial loops.
    \item \textbf{Single Point of Failure and Surveillance Target}: Maintaining a centralized telemetry repository establishes an attractive single point of compromise, allowing an adversary who breaches the central store to inspect complete internal communication patterns.
    \item \textbf{Breach of Data Privacy and Sovereignty Mandates}: Exporting raw NetFlow or IPFIX records across administrative boundaries risks violating regulatory frameworks such as GDPR, HIPAA, and CCPA. Flow attributes expose private subnet topologies, server port allocations, and workstation activity timelines, creating organizational compliance liabilities.
\end{enumerate}

To reconcile collaborative model training with institutional data containment, Federated Learning (FL) establishes a framework where participating edge clients optimize a shared model collaboratively without centralizing raw traffic logs~\cite{mcmahan2017federated}. Under the Federated Averaging (\texttt{FedAvg}) protocol introduced by McMahan et al. (2017), client nodes train local models on private flow captures and upload only parameter weights or gradient updates to an aggregation coordinator. The coordinator synthesizes these contributions into a consensus global parameter vector, which is distributed back to edge nodes for local intrusion classification.

Simultaneously, the cybersecurity research community has increasingly recognized that network communication events are inherently relational and topological rather than isolated, independent tabular records. Standard classifiers typically treat flow records as independent feature vectors, omitting the rich topological interactions that govern host-to-host conversations. However, network intrusions---including distributed denial-of-service, multi-target port scanning, botnet beaconing, and lateral movement---exhibit explicit relational topologies governed by source-destination IP interactions, communication density, and multi-hop routing paths. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)---such as Graph Attention Networks (GAT) and GraphSAGE---natively represent these relational dynamics by treating IP addresses as graph vertices and NetFlow sessions as attributed directed edges, extracting structural interdependencies that flat tabular models overlook~\cite{velickovic2018gat, hamilton2017graphsage}.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}

Although integrating Federated Learning with Graph Neural Networks provides clear privacy and structural benefits, existing FedGNN intrusion detection designs face four major methodological and structural bottlenecks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pervasive Socket-Signature Evaluation Data Leakage in Academic Benchmarks}: Many intrusion detection evaluations rely on uniform random train-test row splits. In flow-based datasets, repeated packets from a single attack run or application session generate identical socket signatures (source/destination IPs, Layer 4 ports, transport protocol, and flow metrics). Standard row splitting allocates records from the same flow signature into both training and evaluation subsets simultaneously. This allows models to achieve high apparent test accuracy ($>99\%$) through memorizing static socket combinations, hiding severe performance degradation on genuinely unseen sessions.
    \item \textbf{Catastrophic Minority Class Collapse Under Empirical Risk Minimization}: Real-world network traffic distributions are characterized by acute, long-tail class imbalance. Benign background transactions and high-volume volumetric attacks (such as DDoS and Injection) outnumber critical targeted intrusions (such as stealthy Backdoors and administrative password guessing) by multiple orders of magnitude (exceeding a $27:1$ ratio in benchmarks like NF-ToN-IoT). Under standard cross-entropy loss optimized via empirical risk minimization, gradient update trajectories are overwhelmingly dictated by majority-class sample volumes. Consequently, the decision boundaries of rare minority attacks are completely subsumed by adjacent majority manifolds, precipitating intolerable False Negative Rates (FNR) exceeding $99\%$ on the most damaging and stealthy network intrusions.
    \item \textbf{Topological Feature Blurring and Oversmoothing on Multi-Role Endpoint Nodes}: In graph representations of enterprise network telemetry, IP endpoint nodes routinely serve dual operational roles, concurrently participating in legitimate high-volume transactions and rare malicious flow exchanges. Standard GNN message-passing formulations initialize node representations by computing the mean of all incident flow attributes. When low-volume stealth attacks (such as a C2 backdoor) traverse an endpoint that simultaneously handles massive volumes of background or web injection traffic, recursive neighborhood feature averaging severely dilutes and erases the subtle minority attack signature into the dominant host profile. This topological oversmoothing causes continuous edge embeddings to collapse entirely into adjacent majority clusters.
    \item \textbf{Structural Incoherence from Disconnected Heuristic Graph Partitions}: Existing federated graph systems often utilize Louvain modularity clustering to abstract client subgraphs before transmitting representations to the coordinator. However, Louvain's heuristic aggregation can produce communities that are internally disconnected or weakly cohesive. Forwarding the pooled centroids of fragmented subgraphs introduces structural noise into the server's inter-community overlay, impeding GraphSAGE aggregation across clients.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives}\label{sec:objectives}

To resolve these methodological and structural limitations, this investigation designs and validates a contrastive hybrid federated GNN framework tailored for highly skewed IoT traffic telemetry. The study pursues the following core technical objectives:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Formulation of an Audited Zero-Leakage Benchmark Protocol}: Implement and verify a 5-fold \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} partitioning scheme grouped on an 8-tuple socket signature across 1,377,837 NetFlow records in NF-ToN-IoT, confirming $0.00\%$ signature leakage between folds to ensure realistic evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Engineering of Domain-Specialized Client GAT Subgraphs}: Construct three specialized Graph Attention Network (GAT) encoders---Temporal, Content, and Behavioral---to decouple dynamic flow duration, payload volumetric asymmetry, and port protocol behaviors without inter-feature interference.
    \item \textbf{Integration of Leiden Constant Potts Model Community Partitioning}: Apply the Leiden algorithm under the Constant Potts Model (CPM) to partition client graphs, ensuring discovered communities remain strictly connected and structurally well-formed prior to server pooling.
    \item \textbf{Formulation of Supervised Contrastive Metric Regularization (SupCon)}: Formulate a dual-objective training loss combining Class-Balanced Focal loss ($\beta=0.9999, \gamma=2.0$) with Supervised Contrastive (SupCon) regularization ($\mu=0.1, \tau=0.07$) on normalized flow embeddings, generating geometric repulsion between overlapping attack classes.
    \item \textbf{Implementation of Multi-Tiered Class Balancing Strategies}: Integrate client-level intra-class convex interpolation and dynamic batch sampling ($M=64$ edges per class per step) to provide balanced gradient signals under severe label scarcity.
    \item \textbf{Design of a Two-Stage Random Forest Meta-Learner}: Develop an ensemble Random Forest meta-classifier that arbitrates conflicting continuous GNN detector output probabilities by combining them with normalized raw physical NetFlow attribute thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Seed Statistical Replication and Diagnostic Auditing}: Evaluate the complete system across multiple random initialization seeds (seeds 42--44 on NF-ToN-IoT and seeds 42--46 on NF-UNSW-NB15) to ensure statistical rigor and empirically trace the mechanism behind minority-class recovery.
\end{itemize}

\section{Comprehensive Survey}\label{sec:comprehensive_survey}
\label{sec:survey}

Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) operating within distributed Internet of Things (IoT) ecosystems have evolved through multiple distinct paradigms over the past two decades. Modern intrusion detection sits at the convergence of four major foundational domains: decentralized federated protocols, graph neural architectures for network telemetry, loss formulations for heavy class imbalance, and contrastive metric learning. This section reviews core theoretical foundations and recent empirical studies across each area.

\subsection{Classical Machine Learning and Deep Learning in Intrusion Detection}
Early research in automated network intrusion detection focused predominantly on signature-based matching algorithms and classical shallow machine learning classifiers. Signature-based intrusion detection tools (such as Snort and Suricata) match packet payloads against rulesets of known attack fingerprints. While fast and precise for recognized exploits, signature approaches struggle against polymorphic variants, evasion techniques, and zero-day threats. To improve detection breadth, research transitioned toward statistical and machine learning classifiers trained on flow summaries, including Random Forests~\cite{breiman2001randomforests}, Support Vector Machines (SVMs), and $k$-Nearest Neighbors ($k$-NN).

Random Forest models in particular have shown strong baseline efficacy on tabular benchmark datasets such as KDD-Cup 99 and NSL-KDD. By aggregating decision trees over bootstrap samples with random feature selection, tree ensembles form sharp, axis-aligned boundaries that handle noisy tabular metrics effectively. Nonetheless, standard tabular classifiers require aggregating data into a centralized store, raising regulatory and privacy challenges, and depend heavily on feature engineering without capturing host communication structure.

With the advent of deep learning, researchers explored deep feedforward networks, Deep Autoencoders, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), including Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), to analyze traffic streams. Deep autoencoders learn normal baseline reconstructions to flag deviations, while recurrent models capture sequence dynamics in packet arrival timing. Even so, these deep architectures largely process flows as independent records, neglecting host-to-host interaction graphs and hindering the correlation of coordinated reconnaissance or lateral movement across enterprise segments.

\subsection{Federated Learning Paradigms in Network Security}
To support multi-institutional model training under privacy requirements such as GDPR and HIPAA, Federated Learning (FL) coordinates decentralized model optimization without transferring raw network traffic~\cite{mcmahan2017federated}. Under the standard Federated Averaging (\texttt{FedAvg}) method, a coordination server orchestrates iterative training rounds: participating clients download current global weights, update them locally via stochastic gradient descent on private data, and report their updated parameters back to the server. The central coordinator then averages client weights based on sample size:
\begin{equation}
W_{t+1} = \sum_{k=1}^K \left( \frac{n_k}{n} \right) W_{t+1}^k
\label{eq:fedavg_theory}
\end{equation}
Exchanging model weights rather than flow logs substantially lowers wide-area bandwidth consumption while preventing direct exposure of internal host identifiers.

However, real-world edge IoT networks present non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) conditions that degrade standard federated workflows. Zhao et al. (2018) showed that distribution divergence among clients induces parameter drift during local training epochs, slowing global convergence and degrading test performance~\cite{zhao2018federated}. To stabilize training across heterogeneous clients, Li et al. (2020) proposed \texttt{FedProx}, adding a proximal penalty that limits local model deviations from the global consensus~\cite{li2020federatedchallenges}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_{\text{prox}} = \frac{\mu}{2} \left\| W - W_t \right\|^2
\end{equation}

In network intrusion detection contexts, statistical heterogeneity is exacerbated by the highly specialized nature of IoT deployments. An edge gateway supervising environmental industrial sensors experiences a traffic profile radically different from an administrative edge router handling corporate communications. Certain intrusion types---such as industrial protocol manipulation or targeted backdoor beaconing---may appear only on specific subnets while remaining unobserved by others. Addressing this client-level imbalance, Youm and Kim (2025) introduced \texttt{DS-FedIDS}, which dynamically adjusts client loss weights according to local class representations~\cite{youm2025dsfedids}. Partitioning with Dirichlet distributions $\text{Dir}(\alpha)$ has likewise become standard for modeling realistic client label skew~\cite{hsu2019noniid}. Even so, conventional aggregation often degrades when rare intrusions are entirely absent from most participating nodes.

\subsection{Graph Neural Networks for Relational Network Telemetry}
Recognizing that cyber attacks are inherently relational events executed over structured communication topologies, the security community has increasingly embraced Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). Unlike Euclidean convolutional networks designed for regular grids (e.g., images), GNNs operate over arbitrary non-Euclidean graphs $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ by executing recursive neighborhood message-passing convolutions. In cybersecurity graph formulations, unique IP addresses form vertices $\mathcal{V}$, while individual NetFlow communication records form directed attributed edges $\mathcal{E}$.

Graph Attention Networks (GAT), proposed by Veli\v{c}kovi\'{c} et al. (2018), introduced learnable self-attention over node neighborhoods~\cite{velickovic2018gat}. Instead of relying on static degree normalizations (as in standard Graph Convolutional Networks), GAT computes dynamic pairwise attention coefficients $\alpha_{uv}$ reflecting neighbor relevance. In network telemetry, this attention mechanism allows encoders to focus on anomalous or bursty edge exchanges while suppressing routine background traffic.

To scale representation learning to large evolving topologies, Hamilton et al. (2017) designed GraphSAGE, an inductive framework that samples localized neighborhoods and pools features through learnable aggregators~\cite{hamilton2017graphsage}. Because it learns aggregation functions rather than fixed node embeddings, GraphSAGE readily generalizes to newly observed network hosts without retraining the full graph topology.

Several researchers have tailored GNN architectures specifically to edge-attributed network telemetry. Lo et al. (2022) developed E-GraphSAGE, extending GraphSAGE to handle flow-specific attributes directly during message aggregation~\cite{lo2022egraphsage}. Chang and Branco (2021) introduced E-ResGAT, combining residual connections with edge-aware attention to retain flow statistics and prevent feature oversmoothing across dense nodes~\cite{chang2021eresgat}. Similarly, Han et al. (2025) proposed FedSTGCN, coupling spatiotemporal graph convolutions with federated learning to capture temporal bursts across IoT edge devices~\cite{han2025fedstgcn}. As highlighted in a recent survey by Liu et al. (2024), scaling federated GNNs under extreme class imbalance and non-IID graph topologies remains an open challenge~\cite{liu2024fedgnn}.

\subsection{Class Imbalance and Optimization Loss Formulations}
Network intrusion benchmarks are notoriously characterized by extreme class imbalance. Normal background flows commonly account for $80\%$ to $90\%$ of records, volumetric attacks (such as DDoS or web injection) comprise moderate shares ($10\%$ to $20\%$), and targeted intrusions (such as backdoor persistence, brute-force password guessing, and shellcode exploits) make up less than $1.5\%$ of total traffic~\cite{sarhan2021netflow, alsaedi2020toniot}. As Johnson and Khoshgoftaar (2019) documented, optimizing standard unweighted cross-entropy loss under severe skew causes gradient updates to be dominated by high-frequency majority flows~\cite{johnson2019survey}. Consequently, the decision boundaries of rare attacks are easily compromised, leading to elevated False Negative Rates (FNR) on critical security threats.

To avoid data loss from undersampling, loss-reweighting and focal modulation techniques have been widely studied. Lin et al. (2017) formulated Focal Loss, applying a modulating term $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$ to standard cross-entropy~\cite{lin2017focal}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FL}} = - (1 - p_t)^\gamma \log(p_t)
\end{equation}
For confident predictions ($p_t \to 1$, common among majority benign traffic), this modulating term downscales gradient magnitude, redirecting model capacity toward ambiguous boundary examples and rare minority instances.

Addressing the diminishing returns of redundant instances, Cui et al. (2019) formulated Class-Balanced Loss using effective sample volume theory~\cite{cui2019cbloss}. By modeling data overlap, the effective sample volume is defined as:
\begin{equation}
E_n = \frac{1 - \beta^n}{1 - \beta}, \quad \text{where } \beta \in [0, 1)
\end{equation}
Weighting loss terms inversely by effective volume rather than raw frequencies:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_c \propto \frac{1 - \beta}{1 - \beta^{n_c}}
\end{equation}
prevents dominant classes from driving the gradient while avoiding excessive penalties on moderate classes. At the data level, Zhang et al. (2018) proposed Mixup, generating virtual examples via convex feature interpolation~\cite{zhang2018mixup}:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x} = \lambda x_i + (1 - \lambda) x_j
\end{equation}
which regularizes neural networks by encouraging smoother transition regions between classes.

\subsection{Contrastive Representation Learning}
Contrastive representation learning structures latent spaces by drawing embeddings of similar samples closer while repelling dissimilar samples. In self-supervised frameworks such as SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020) and MoCo (He et al., 2020), positive pairs originate from different augmentations of the same unlabeled sample, while all other batch elements act as negative distractors.

Khosla et al. (2020) generalized this concept to labeled datasets through Supervised Contrastive Learning (SupCon)~\cite{khosla2020supcon}. In SupCon, all instances sharing a class label within a mini-batch serve as mutual positives, while instances with differing labels act as negatives. Khosla et al. demonstrated that SupCon yields clearer angular separation between class manifolds and demonstrates greater stability than unweighted cross-entropy. However, incorporating SupCon as an architectural regularizer in federated GNNs to counteract topological dilution on skewed NetFlow data remains largely unexplored.

\subsection{Critical Re-Examination and Audit of the Base FedGATSage Framework}
This research builds upon the FedGATSage pipeline introduced by Al-Tfaily et al. (2025) in Nature Scientific Reports~\cite{altfaily2025fedgatsage}. FedGATSage combines client-side Graph Attention Networks (GAT), Louvain community clustering, an aggregated server GraphSAGE overlay, and a Random Forest meta-learner, reporting a Balanced Accuracy of $78.58\%$ and Macro FNR of $22.34\%$ on NF-ToN-IoT. However, an empirical code audit uncovered several methodological and structural limitations in this baseline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Catastrophic Minority Class Collapse Under Empirical Risk Minimization}: In the base FedGATSage pipeline, local client GAT models are trained strictly using unweighted Cross-Entropy loss. Our multi-seed empirical replication on the official NF-ToN-IoT benchmark revealed that the reported $22.34\%$ Macro FNR is driven almost entirely by the near-total collapse of rare attack classes. Specifically, for the stealthy Backdoor class ($3,436$ test flows), the baseline anchor achieves an appalling recall of only $0.11 \pm 0.08\%$ across three random seeds. Confusion matrix auditing revealed that $99.83\%$ ($3,430$ out of $3,436$) of Backdoor flows are erroneously classified as Injection traffic. Because Injection outnumbers Backdoor by $27:1$ in training ($374,831$ vs. $13,797$ flows), cross-entropy gradient descent allows the Injection class manifold to swallow the Backdoor boundary.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation Data Leakage in Benchmark Row Splitting}: In the published FedGATSage study, the experimental evaluation utilized a naive uniform random row split of NetFlow records. Our empirical leakage audit revealed that this random split leaked $46.88\%$ of identical flow communication signatures between the training and testing partitions. Consequently, the baseline model achieved artificially inflated detection accuracy by memorizing recurring socket identifiers, masking its catastrophic failure on unseen network sessions.
    \item \textbf{Topological Incoherence from Louvain Partitioning}: FedGATSage relies on the Louvain algorithm to partition client graphs into communities. As Traag et al. (2019) established, Louvain clustering can yield communities containing disconnected components~\cite{traag2019leiden}. Passing centroids from fragmented subgraphs into the server overlay introduces geometric noise during cross-client GraphSAGE aggregation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Open Research Challenges}\label{sec:challenges}

Reviewing existing literature reveals four primary technical bottlenecks that limit the operational reliability of federated graph intrusion detection in IoT deployments:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Evaluation Leakage Blindness}: Many published NIDS studies evaluate models using uniform random row splits. Because repeated packets in a single communication burst share identical socket identifiers, random partitioning allows models to memorize static flow tuples, inflating evaluation metrics while obscuring failure on novel sessions.
    \item \textbf{Continuous Manifold Overlap}: Whereas decision tree algorithms partition feature space with orthogonal threshold planes, neural network encoders project flows into continuous latent embeddings. Under severe class imbalance, majority-class gradients dominate parameter updates, absorbing minority-class manifolds into adjacent majority clusters.
    \item \textbf{Topological Feature Blurring}: GNN convolutions pool adjacent edge attributes onto endpoint vertices. When a network host handles high-volume benign traffic alongside sparse attack flows, neighborhood averaging dilutes rare attack features into the dominant host profile.
    \item \textbf{Client-to-Server Communication Constraints}: Transmitting complete edge graphs from distributed IoT clients creates unsustainable communication overhead. Effective community abstraction is necessary to compress local topologies without erasing relational indicators.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Motivation of the Project Work}\label{sec:motivation}

Motivated by these vulnerabilities in the base FedGATSage model and wider literature, this dissertation investigates a mathematically regularized and empirically audited federated intrusion detection framework. We hypothesize that introducing Supervised Contrastive Loss into local graph encoders creates an active metric repulsive force that prevents minority-manifold collapse under severe class skew. By conducting all evaluations under an audited zero-leakage signature-grouped protocol across multiple random seeds, this research demonstrates that federated privacy and high minority-class recall can be achieved simultaneously.

\section{Methods of Data Processing and Pre-processing}\label{sec:preprocessing}

To transform raw, unformatted NetFlow records into structurally consistent, normalized graph representations suitable for federated training, our data processing pipeline executes four systematic pre-processing stages: attribute sanitization, RFC 793 bitwise TCP flag decoding, logarithmic feature standardization, and domain-specialized feature partitioning.

\subsection{Data Ingestion and RFC 793 TCP Flag Bitwise Decoding}
Raw flow telemetry stores cumulative TCP control flags as an aggregate integer (\texttt{TCP\_FLAGS}) computed via the bitwise OR of individual packet headers. In this raw form, connection handshake states are obscured: for instance, a value of 18 indicates $\text{SYN} + \text{ACK}$ ($16 + 2$), while 24 denotes $\text{PSH} + \text{ACK}$ ($16 + 8$). Interpreting \texttt{TCP\_FLAGS} as a continuous scalar imposes artificial metric distances that distort neural network learning.

To represent transport-layer protocol semantics explicitly, we extract binary flag indicators following RFC 793 standards~\cite{postel1981tcp}, decomposing the integer into six distinct indicator flags:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FIN} (bit 0, mask \texttt{0x01}): Connection termination indicator
    \item \textbf{SYN} (bit 1, mask \texttt{0x02}): Synchronization handshake initiator
    \item \textbf{RST} (bit 2, mask \texttt{0x04}): Connection reset notification
    \item \textbf{PSH} (bit 3, mask \texttt{0x08}): Immediate application buffer push
    \item \textbf{ACK} (bit 4, mask \texttt{0x10}): Acknowledgment verification flag
    \item \textbf{URG} (bit 5, mask \texttt{0x20}): Urgent pointer data indicator
\end{itemize}
This bitwise decoding enables our GNN encoders to directly identify reconnaissance SYN scans, RST tear-down floods, and PSH payload bursts.

\subsection{Feature Scaling and Domain-Specialized Feature Engineering}
Continuous attributes including \texttt{IN\_BYTES}, \texttt{OUT\_BYTES}, \texttt{IN\_PKTS}, \texttt{OUT\_PKTS}, and \texttt{FLOW\_DURATION\_MS} exhibit heavy right-skewed distributions spanning multiple orders of magnitude. To stabilize gradient descent, we log-transform and standardize each continuous feature:
\begin{equation}
x_{\text{scaled}} = \log(x + 1), \quad z = \frac{x_{\text{scaled}} - \mu}{\sigma}
\end{equation}
with scaling parameters $\mu$ and $\sigma$ estimated exclusively on training splits to prevent evaluation leakage.

Instead of passing all heterogeneous attributes through a single monolithic encoder, we partition features into three specialized subsets corresponding to distinct traffic properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Temporal Feature Set (21 features)}: Captures dynamic packet and byte transmission rates, flow duration, and directional packet ratios, designed to isolate volumetric floods such as DDoS and DoS.
    \item \textbf{Content Feature Set (22 features)}: Encodes payload asymmetries, average packet sizes (\texttt{IN\_BYTES} / \texttt{IN\_PKTS}), byte asymmetry ratios (\texttt{IN\_BYTES} / (\texttt{OUT\_BYTES} + 1)), SYN-to-packet ratio, and ACK proportions. This detector specializes in detecting web injection and XSS.
    \item \textbf{Behavioral Feature Set (21 features)}: Encodes service port privilege indicators (ports $< 1024$), ephemeral port dispersion, transport protocol identifiers, and TCP flag combinations. This detector specializes in isolating administrative password guessing, backdoor beacons, and port scans.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Graph Topological Construction}
From each client's flow logs, we construct a directed communication graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \boldsymbol{X})$. Vertices $\mathcal{V} = \{v_0, \dots, v_{N-1}\}$ correspond to observed IPv4 host addresses, while directed edges $(u, v) \in \mathcal{E}$ represent NetFlow sessions from source to destination. Edge feature vectors $\boldsymbol{e}_{uv} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e}$ store normalized domain-specific attributes. Because standard GAT convolutions initiate message passing at vertices, initial node representations $\boldsymbol{x}_u \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e}$ are computed by averaging incident edge features:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{x}_u = \frac{1}{\text{deg}(u)} \sum_{(u, v) \in \mathcal{E}} \boldsymbol{e}_{uv}
\label{eq:node_init}
\end{equation}
where $\text{deg}(u)$ denotes the combined degree of vertex $u$. Host IP strings are re-indexed to integer identifiers ($0$ to $N-1$) and excluded from model inputs to preserve topological generalizability.

\subsection{Audited Signature-Grouped Split Protocol (Zero Leakage Proof)}
To rigorously eliminate evaluation data leakage, we define an 8-feature communication socket signature $\mathcal{S}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{S} \equiv \big[ & \text{L4\_SRC\_PORT}, \, \text{L4\_DST\_PORT}, \, \text{PROTOCOL}, \, \text{IN\_BYTES}, \\
& \text{OUT\_BYTES}, \, \text{IN\_PKTS}, \, \text{OUT\_PKTS}, \, \text{FLOW\_DURATION} \big]
\end{split}
\label{eq:signature}
\end{equation}
When automated attack scripts execute, consecutive packets generate identical socket signatures. If a benchmark utilizes uniform random row splitting, flows sharing identical signatures are assigned simultaneously to both training and test sets.

\section{Design / Methodology 1 of Existing Work: Centralized Baselines}\label{sec:existing1}
\label{sec:methodology1}

To establish a rigorous comparative foundation for evaluating our proposed architecture, we first analyze the methodological designs of existing conventional approaches in network intrusion detection: centralized tabular classifiers and standard federated deep learning models.

\textbf{Centralized Tabular Architectures}: Standard machine learning NIDS approaches commonly employ tabular ensemble classifiers, such as Random Forests~\cite{breiman2001randomforests} or gradient-boosted trees, trained on consolidated NetFlow repositories. In these setups, each flow is modeled as an independent vector $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^D$. Decision trees recursively partition the input space by minimizing node impurity along individual feature dimensions:
\begin{equation}
\theta^* = \arg\min_{j, t} \left[ \frac{N_L}{N} G(\mathcal{D}_L) + \frac{N_R}{N} G(\mathcal{D}_R) \right]
\end{equation}
where threshold $t$ on feature $j$ splits dataset $\mathcal{D}$ into subsets $\mathcal{D}_L$ and $\mathcal{D}_R$. While axis-aligned splits effectively detect volumetric anomalies governed by clear numerical thresholds (such as packet bursts), centralized tabular approaches face two fundamental limitations: (1) They necessitate pooling unencrypted flow records at a central server, conflicting with privacy frameworks; and (2) They disregard graph relational context, making them less capable of detecting multi-host reconnaissance or distributed lateral movement across subnets.

\textbf{Standard Federated NIDS Architectures}: To preserve privacy, federated intrusion detection architectures typically combine the Federated Averaging (\texttt{FedAvg}) protocol with multi-layer perceptrons or 1D convolutional models. Participating clients compute gradient updates locally using standard cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = - \sum_{c=1}^C y_c \log(p_c)
\end{equation}
Across communication rounds, the central server aggregates client weights weighted by local dataset volumes:
\begin{equation}
W_{\text{global}} = \sum_{k=1}^K \left( \frac{n_k}{n} \right) W_k
\end{equation}
However, typical federated setups still model traffic as independent tabular instances. Under non-IID client skew, local parameter updates diverge along incompatible trajectories. More critically, when rare intrusions (such as Backdoors) are absent from certain clients, \texttt{FedAvg} parameter averaging heavily dilutes minority class weights, causing severe global false negative rates.

\section{Design / Methodology 2 of Existing Work: Base FedGATSage Framework}\label{sec:existing2}
\label{sec:methodology2}

The base FedGATSage architecture proposed by Al-Tfaily et al. (2025) sought to bridge the gap between federated privacy and relational modeling by uniting client-side Graph Attention Networks (GAT), Louvain community detection, an aggregated server GraphSAGE overlay, and a Random Forest meta-classifier~\cite{altfaily2025fedgatsage}. In FedGATSage, each edge client constructs a local communication graph $\mathcal{G}_k = (\mathcal{V}_k, \mathcal{E}_k)$ where vertices represent IP hosts and directed edges represent network flows. Node feature vectors are initialized via incident edge feature averaging:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{x}_u = \frac{1}{\text{deg}(u)} \sum_{(u, v) \in \mathcal{E}} \boldsymbol{e}_{uv}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Client Representation Learning}: Local client training in FedGATSage employs a two-layer Graph Attention Network (GAT) with multi-head attention. For nodes $u$ and $v$, attention coefficients $\alpha_{uv}$ are computed via LeakyReLU-activated linear projections, and node representations are updated across $M$ heads:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{h}_u = \sigma\left( \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \sum_{v \in \mathcal{N}(u)} \alpha_{uv}^m \boldsymbol{W}^m \boldsymbol{x}_v \right)
\end{equation}
Edge representations $\boldsymbol{z}_{uv}$ for flow classification are synthesized by concatenating endpoint representations into an edge MLP:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{z}_{uv} = \text{MLP}_{\text{edge}}\left([\boldsymbol{h}_u \,\|\, \boldsymbol{h}_v]\right)
\end{equation}
Crucially, the base FedGATSage framework optimizes local GAT parameters strictly using standard, unweighted Cross-Entropy empirical risk minimization.

\textbf{Community Detection and Server Overlay}: To preserve client privacy and reduce communication overhead, FedGATSage applies the Louvain modularity algorithm to partition client graphs into community clusters $\mathcal{C}_1, \dots, \mathcal{C}_P$ by optimizing graph modularity:
\begin{equation}
Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{ij} \left[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \right] \delta(c_i, c_j)
\end{equation}
Community centroids $\boldsymbol{c}_p$ are computed by degree-weighted averaging of constituent node embeddings and transmitted to the central server. The server constructs an inter-community overlay graph by connecting community centroids whose pairwise cosine similarity exceeds a dynamic threshold decay heuristic (decaying from 0.7 to 0.3 with minimum degree 3). The server processes this overlay graph using GraphSAGE convolutions, and global parameters are aggregated via a performance-weighted averaging scheme. Finally, an ensemble Random Forest meta-classifier trained on the concatenated probability outputs of the detectors ($3 \times C$) outputs the final class assignment.

\section{Proposed Design and Mathematical Methodology}\label{sec:proposed_method}
\label{sec:formulations}

This section presents the mathematical formulations that define the client encoders, loss objectives, server overlay, and downstream arbitration layers.

\subsection{Three Domain-Specialized Client GAT Detectors}
To isolate orthogonal attack signatures without cross-feature interference, each client $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ trains three specialized Graph Attention Network (GAT) models dedicated to distinct feature domains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Temporal GAT ($\mathcal{M}_{\text{temp}}$)}: Specializes in flow duration, packet arrival rates, byte rates, and forward-to-backward packet ratios.
    \item \textbf{Content GAT ($\mathcal{M}_{\text{cont}}$)}: Specializes in payload volume asymmetry, average packet sizes, and TCP flag distribution ratios.
    \item \textbf{Behavioral GAT ($\mathcal{M}_{\text{behav}}$)}: Specializes in service port privilege flags, ephemeral port dispersion, and protocol identifiers.
\end{itemize}

For each specialized detector $d \in \{\text{temp}, \text{cont}, \text{behav}\}$, local telemetry is mapped to a client subgraph $\mathcal{G}_k^{(d)} = (\mathcal{V}_k, \mathcal{E}_k, \boldsymbol{X}_k^{(d)})$. Initial vertex representations $\boldsymbol{x}_u^{(d)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{feat}}}$ are computed via incident edge averaging: $\boldsymbol{x}_u^{(d)} = \frac{1}{\text{deg}(u)} \sum_{(u, v) \in \mathcal{E}} \boldsymbol{e}_{uv}^{(d)}$. The graph attention layer computes dynamic attention coefficients $\alpha_{uv}$ between connected nodes $u$ and $v$ across $M = 8$ attention heads:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{uv}^{(m)} = \frac{\exp\left( \text{LeakyReLU}\left( \boldsymbol{a}^T [\boldsymbol{W}^{(m)}\boldsymbol{x}_u \,\|\, \boldsymbol{W}^{(m)}\boldsymbol{x}_v] \right) \right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{w \in \mathcal{N}(u)} \exp\left( \text{LeakyReLU}\left( \boldsymbol{a}^T [\boldsymbol{W}^{(m)}\boldsymbol{x}_u \,\|\, \boldsymbol{W}^{(m)}\boldsymbol{x}_w] \right) \right)}
\label{eq:gat_attn_heads}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{W}^{(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^{32 \times d_{\text{feat}}}$ is a learnable projection matrix for head $m$, $\boldsymbol{a} \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$ is a learnable attention parameter vector, $\|$ denotes vector concatenation, and $\mathcal{N}(u)$ represents the immediate neighborhood of host $u$.

Vertex embeddings are updated by aggregating projected neighbor features weighted by attention coefficients across all $M = 8$ heads, followed by ELU activation:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{h}_u^{(d)} = \text{ELU}\left( \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \sum_{v \in \mathcal{N}(u)} \alpha_{uv}^{(m)} \boldsymbol{W}^{(m)} \boldsymbol{x}_v \right)
\label{eq:gat_node_update}
\end{equation}
yielding a 256-dimensional latent representation for each host node. A second GAT layer applies identical attention convolutions: $\boldsymbol{h}_u'^{(d)} = \text{GATLayer}_2(\boldsymbol{h}_u^{(d)})$.

Because NetFlow intrusion detection requires classifying directional flows (edges) rather than individual host vertices, edge representations $\boldsymbol{z}_{uv}^{(d)}$ are synthesized by feeding concatenated endpoint node embeddings into a dedicated two-layer Edge Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP):
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\boldsymbol{z}_{uv}^{(d)} = \text{Linear}_{256 \to 8}\Big( & \text{Dropout}_{0.2}\big( \text{ReLU}\big( \\
& \text{Linear}_{512 \to 256}\left( [\boldsymbol{h}_u'^{(d)} \,\|\, \boldsymbol{h}_v'^{(d)}] \right) \big) \big) \Big)
\end{split}
\label{eq:edge_mlp_layer}
\end{equation}
where the 256-dimensional intermediate layer $\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{uv}^{(d)}$ serves as the continuous edge embedding used for contrastive regularization, and the final 8-dimensional output vector produces pre-softmax detector classification logits: $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{uv}^{(d)} = \text{Softmax}(\boldsymbol{z}_{uv}^{(d)})$.

\subsection{Leiden Community Partitioning and Centroid Pooling}
To eliminate the disconnected community artifacts generated by Louvain modularity optimization in base architectures, our framework incorporates the Leiden community detection algorithm~\cite{traag2019leiden}. The Leiden algorithm operates by optimizing the Constant Potts Model (CPM) quality function $\mathcal{H}_{\text{CPM}}$ parameterized by resolution parameter $\gamma = 1.0$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}_{\text{CPM}} = \sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \left[ e(c, c) - \gamma \binom{n_c}{2} \right]
\label{eq:cpm_quality}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}$ denotes the partition of vertices into communities, $e(c, c)$ is the total edge weight internal to community $c$, and $n_c$ is the number of nodes in $c$. The Leiden algorithm guarantees well-connected communities by executing three alternating phases: (1) Local movement of individual nodes; (2) Refinement of the discovered partition where communities are iteratively split into connected components; and (3) Graph aggregation based on the refined partition. This guarantees that every discovered community is strictly connected.

For each detected community $c_p \in \mathcal{C}_k$, client $k$ synthesizes a community centroid embedding $\boldsymbol{c}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$ via uniform mean pooling over all constituent host nodes:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{c}_p = \frac{1}{|V_p|} \sum_{u \in V_p} \boldsymbol{h}_u'
\label{eq:centroid_pool}
\end{equation}
Community centroid pooling compresses local client telemetry by $85\%$ (reducing transmission from $\sim 25\text{ KB}$ of raw telemetry to $3.2\text{ KB}$ of centroid vectors per client), preserving client identity privacy while abstracting topological communication patterns for server aggregation.

\subsection{Class-Balanced Focal Optimization}
To mitigate extreme majority-class dominance during client training, local GAT parameters are optimized using Class-Balanced Focal (CB-Focal) Loss~\cite{cui2019cbloss, lin2017focal}. Based on effective sample volume theory, the effective number of training instances $E_{n_y}$ for class $y$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
E_{n_y} = \frac{1 - \beta^{n_y}}{1 - \beta}
\label{eq:eff_samples}
\end{equation}
where $n_y$ is the sample count for class $y$, and $\beta = 0.9999$ controls the effective volume growth rate. The normalized class-balancing weight $\alpha_y$ is:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_y = \frac{1 - \beta}{1 - \beta^{n_y}} \cdot \left( \sum_{c=1}^C \frac{1 - \beta}{1 - \beta^{n_c}} \right)^{-1} \cdot C
\label{eq:cb_weights_def}
\end{equation}

This class-balancing weight is embedded into the Focal Loss formulation with focusing parameter $\gamma = 2.0$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CB-Focal}} = - \alpha_y (1 - p_t)^\gamma \log(p_t)
\label{eq:cb_focal_eq}
\end{equation}
where $p_t$ is the predicted probability for the target class. Focal modulation suppresses the loss gradients of confident majority samples ($p_t \to 1$), ensuring that backpropagation focuses primarily on boundary and hard-to-classify flows.

\subsection{Supervised Contrastive Representation Regularization (SupCon)}
Although CB-Focal loss dynamically scales gradient magnitudes, it does not directly optimize the angular separation of embeddings in continuous latent space. Under extreme $27:1$ class imbalance, cross-entropy derivatives still allow majority clusters to swallow minority decision boundaries. To establish active angular metric separation, we formulate Supervised Contrastive (SupCon) loss~\cite{khosla2020supcon} directly on normalized edge representations: $\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i = \boldsymbol{z}_i / \|\boldsymbol{z}_i\|_2$.

Within each mini-batch $I$ of edge indices, the multi-view Supervised Contrastive loss with temperature $\tau = 0.07$ is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}} = \sum_{i \in I} \frac{-1}{|P(i)|} \sum_{p \in P(i)} \log \frac{\exp\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i \cdot \tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_p / \tau\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{a \in A(i)} \exp\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i \cdot \tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_a / \tau\right)}
\label{eq:supcon_main}
\end{equation}
where $A(i) = I \setminus \{i\}$ indexes all mini-batch edges excluding anchor $i$, $P(i) = \{p \in A(i) : y_p = y_i\}$ indexes all co-class positive edges relative to anchor $i$, and $|P(i)|$ is positive pair cardinality.

\textbf{Gradient Dynamics of Contrastive Regularization}: Differentiating $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}}$ with respect to the normalized edge embedding $\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i$ yields:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i} \mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}} = \frac{1}{\tau} \left( \sum_{a \in A(i)} P_{ia} \tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_a - \frac{1}{|P(i)|} \sum_{p \in P(i)} \tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_p \right)
\label{eq:supcon_grad_deriv}
\end{equation}
where $P_{ia} = \exp(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i \cdot \tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_a / \tau) / \sum_{k \in A(i)} \exp(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i \cdot \tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_k / \tau)$. Eq.~\ref{eq:supcon_grad_deriv} reveals the dual geometric forces exerted by SupCon: (1) An attractive force (second term) pulling representations of the same intrusion class together toward their class centroid; and (2) A powerful repulsive force (first term) pushing representations of all non-matching classes apart with magnitude proportional to $-\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_a / \tau$.

The overall local client optimization objective combines CB-Focal classification loss with SupCon representation regularization weighted by hyperparameter $\mu = 0.1$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CB-Focal}} + \mu \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}}
\label{eq:joint_loss}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dynamic Class-Balanced Sampling and Intra-Class Edge Interpolation}
To guarantee that every optimization step contains adequate positive pairs for rare intrusions, a dynamic sampler draws an equal quota of $M = 64$ edges per class per mini-batch step:
\begin{equation}
B_{\text{step}} = \bigcup_{c=1}^C \text{Sample}\left(\{e \in \mathcal{E}_k : y_e = c\}, \min(M, N_c)\right)
\end{equation}
If a rare class contains fewer than $M$ edges in the client graph, replacement sampling is dynamically applied.

Furthermore, to expand the geometric support of sparse minority manifolds, we implement client-local intra-class convex feature interpolation along flow feature dimensions~\cite{zhang2018mixup}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\text{new}} = \lambda \boldsymbol{e}_i + (1 - \lambda) \boldsymbol{e}_j, \quad & \text{where } y_i = y_j = c, \\
& \lambda \sim \text{Beta}(0.5, 0.5)
\end{split}
\label{eq:convex_interpolation}
\end{equation}
where interpolation ratio is fixed at $0.50$ (maximum 10x augmentation). Restricting interpolation strictly within identical class labels prevents the generation of corrupted boundary flows while densifying minority attack manifolds.

\subsection{Server GraphSAGE Overlay and Two-Stage Random Forest Meta-Learner}
At the centralized coordination layer, community centroids $\boldsymbol{c}_p$ received from participating clients form an aggregated server overlay graph $\mathcal{G}_{\text{overlay}} = (\mathcal{V}_{\text{comm}}, \mathcal{E}_{\text{comm}})$. To bound communication density, inter-community edges are established using a static top-$k$ nearest neighbor protocol with $k = 5$ based on pairwise cosine similarity:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_{\text{comm}} = \left\{ (p, q) : q \in \text{top-}k_{r \neq p}\left( \frac{\boldsymbol{c}_p \cdot \boldsymbol{c}_r}{\|\boldsymbol{c}_p\|_2 \|\boldsymbol{c}_r\|_2} \right) \right\}
\label{eq:topk_edges}
\end{equation}
The server executes 2-layer GraphSAGE inductive convolutions over this overlay graph to capture macro-level inter-community relationships across enterprise subnets. Concurrently, local client GAT model parameters are synchronized across communication rounds using edge-count weighted Federated Averaging (\texttt{FedAvg}):
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{global}}^{(d)} = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{|\mathcal{E}_k|}{\sum_{j} |\mathcal{E}_j|} \boldsymbol{\Theta}_k^{(d)}
\label{eq:fedavg_rounds}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Two-Stage Random Forest Meta-Classifier}: To arbitrate final flow classifications, an ensemble Random Forest of 100 balanced trees (maximum depth 20) evaluates a fused feature vector $\boldsymbol{\phi}_i$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\boldsymbol{\phi}_i = \big[ & \boldsymbol{P}_{\text{temp}}(i) \,\|\, \boldsymbol{P}_{\text{cont}}(i) \,\|\, \boldsymbol{P}_{\text{behav}}(i) \,\|\, \\
& \boldsymbol{y}_{\text{pred}} \,\|\, \boldsymbol{conf} \,\|\, \boldsymbol{x}_{\text{raw}}(i) \big]
\end{split}
\label{eq:meta_vec}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{P}_d(i) \in \mathbb{R}^8$ denotes the class probability vector from each GAT model (24 dimensions total), $\boldsymbol{y}_{\text{pred}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ contains individual model argmax predictions, $\boldsymbol{conf} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ stores maximum confidence scores, and $\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{raw}}(i) \in \mathbb{R}^{21}$ provides the normalized NetFlow features. Including raw features alongside model probabilities supplies the tree ensemble with exact continuous thresholds (such as packet arrival rates and byte ratios), resolving ambiguities in continuous GNN logits.

\section{Proposed Complete Flowchart and Algorithmic Workflows}\label{sec:flowchart}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Dynamic Class-Balanced Sampling and SupCon Loss Evaluation}\label{alg:dynamic_sampling}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Client edge set $\mathcal{E}_k$, ground-truth labels $\mathcal{Y}_k$, samples per class $M=64$, temperature $\tau=0.07$, local model $\Theta^{(d)}$.
\Ensure Mini-batch loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}}$ and backpropagated gradients.
\State Initialize empty mini-batch edge index set $\mathcal{B} \gets \emptyset$.
\For{each class $c \in \{1, \dots, C\}$}
    \State Identify edge indices belonging to class $c$: $\mathcal{E}_c = \{e \in \mathcal{E}_k : y_e = c\}$.
    \If{$|\mathcal{E}_c| \ge M$}
        \State Sample $M$ edge indices uniformly at random without replacement from $\mathcal{E}_c$.
        \State $\mathcal{B} \gets \mathcal{B} \cup \text{Sample}(\mathcal{E}_c, M)$.
    \ElsIf{$|\mathcal{E}_c| > 0$}
        \State Sample all available instances $\mathcal{E}_c$.
        \State Synthesize remaining $M - |\mathcal{E}_c|$ virtual edges via intra-class interpolation:
        \State $\tilde{\mathbf{e}} = \lambda \mathbf{e}_i + (1 - \lambda) \mathbf{e}_j, \quad \lambda \sim \text{Beta}(0.5, 0.5), \quad i, j \in \mathcal{E}_c$.
        \State $\mathcal{B} \gets \mathcal{B} \cup \mathcal{E}_c \cup \{\tilde{\mathbf{e}}\}$.
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State Extract node-pair representations $\{\mathbf{z}_i\}_{i \in \mathcal{B}}$ via forward pass through GAT encoder $\Theta^{(d)}$.
\State Normalize representations to unit hypersphere: $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_i = \mathbf{z}_i / \|\mathbf{z}_i\|_2$.
\State Compute Class-Balanced Focal loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CB-Focal}}$ over sampled batch $\mathcal{B}$.
\State Compute Supervised Contrastive regularizer:
\State $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \frac{-1}{|P(i)|} \sum_{p \in P(i)} \log \frac{\exp(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_i \cdot \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_p / \tau)}{\sum_{a \in A(i)} \exp(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_i \cdot \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_a / \tau)}$.
\State Formulate joint objective: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CB-Focal}} + \mu \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}}$.
\State \Return $\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}}$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\label{sec:algorithms}

The end-to-end execution of our contrastive hybrid federated framework is formalized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:fed_pipeline}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Overall Federated Contrastive GNN Training Pipeline}
\label{alg:fed_pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Edge clients $\mathcal{D}_1, \dots, \mathcal{D}_K$, communication rounds $R$, local epochs $E$, temperature $\tau = 0.07$, SupCon weight $\mu = 0.1$.
\Ensure Synchronized global detectors $\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{global}}^{(d)}$ and trained meta-classifier $\mathcal{RF}$.
\STATE Initialize global GAT model weights $\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{global}}^{(d)}$ for $d \in \{\text{temp}, \text{cont}, \text{behav}\}$.
\FOR{federated round $r = 1$ to $R$}
    \FOR{each edge client $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ in parallel}
        \STATE Synchronize local parameters: $\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k, r}^{(d)} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{global}, r-1}^{(d)}$.
        \STATE Construct client graph $\mathcal{G}_k$ with intra-class edge Mixup (Eq.~\ref{eq:convex_interpolation}).
        \STATE Discover communities $\mathcal{C}_k$ via Leiden Constant Potts Model (Eq.~\ref{eq:cpm_quality}).
        \STATE Compute effective sample class weights $\alpha_y$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:cb_weights_def}).
        \FOR{epoch $e = 1$ to $E$}
            \STATE Sample class-balanced mini-batch $B_{\text{step}}$ guaranteeing $M = 64$ samples/class.
            \STATE Compute forward edge embeddings $\boldsymbol{z}_i$ and logits $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_i$.
            \STATE Evaluate CB-Focal loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CB-Focal}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:cb_focal_eq}) and SupCon loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:supcon_main}).
            \STATE Update local weights: $\boldsymbol{\Theta}_k^{(d)} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\Theta}_k^{(d)} - \eta \nabla (\mathcal{L}_{\text{CB-Focal}} + \mu \mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}})$.
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE Pool community centroids $\boldsymbol{c}_p = \frac{1}{|V_p|} \sum_{u \in V_p} \boldsymbol{h}_u'$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:centroid_pool}).
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Transmit centroids to server and construct top-$k$ overlay graph $\mathcal{G}_{\text{overlay}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:topk_edges}).
    \STATE Execute server GraphSAGE message passing over inter-community overlay.
    \STATE Synchronize global parameters via FedAvg: $\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{global}, r}^{(d)} = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{|\mathcal{E}_k|}{\sum_j |\mathcal{E}_j|} \boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k, r}^{(d)}$.
\ENDFOR
\STATE Extract meta-features $\boldsymbol{\phi}_i$ across all validation flows (Eq.~\ref{eq:meta_vec}).
\STATE Fit downstream Random Forest meta-classifier $\mathcal{RF}$ on fused meta-features.
\RETURN Global detectors $\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{global}}^{(d)}$ and ensemble meta-classifier $\mathcal{RF}$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Analytical Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The empirical findings of this dissertation provide fundamental insights into the dynamics of graph representation learning on imbalanced network telemetry. In standard cross-entropy empirical risk minimization, gradient updates are proportional to prediction error:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}}{\partial z_i} = p_i - y_i
\end{equation}
Because volumetric attacks heavily outnumber low-frequency intrusions (over $27:1$), majority-class error terms predominantly guide parameter updates during backpropagation. In continuous latent space, minority attack boundaries are swallowed by majority centroids.

Supervised Contrastive Loss counteracts this gradient dominance by exerting an active repulsive force proportional to $-\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_a / \tau$ between differing classes. When coupled with dynamic class-balanced batching ($M = 64$ samples per class), each step provides balanced positive and negative pairs across all categories. The resulting repulsion isolates minority embeddings from neighboring majority clusters, largely eliminating the misclassification of Backdoors as Injections.

\textbf{Centralized Tabular vs. Federated Graph Trade-Off}: Comparing centralized tabular baselines ($97.53 \pm 0.46\%$ Balanced Accuracy) with the proposed federated GNN ($94.28 \pm 1.20\%$) highlights key operational trade-offs. Centralized models offer high detection precision on known numerical thresholds but require aggregating unencrypted telemetry centrally, conflicting with data privacy regulations such as GDPR and HIPAA. In contrast, the federated GNN retains raw NetFlow data locally, sharing only abstract community centroids. Additionally, while tabular models depend on fixed feature thresholds vulnerable to payload obfuscation, graph architectures capture relational interaction patterns across communication endpoints.

\textbf{Limitations and Threats to Validity}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-Class Concentration}: Exactly $90.85\%$ of the headline gain is driven by rescuing Backdoor intrusions. Volumetric classes were already saturated.
    \item \textbf{High Intra-Class Variance Sensitivity}: Contrastive clustering over-penalizes multi-protocol Password attacks, causing a slight recall degradation.
    \item \textbf{Downstream Meta-Learner Reliance}: Disabling raw flow features in the Random Forest drops BalAcc by $44.60\%$, proving reliance on physical thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Scope of Leakage Control}: The 8-tuple grouped split controls flow-signature leakage, but does not control subnet IP address overlap across folds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion_summary}

This dissertation has presented the design, implementation, mathematical formulation, and multi-seed empirical audit of a contrastive hybrid federated Graph Neural Network (FedGNN) architecture for intrusion detection in decentralized Internet of Things (IoT) networks. Motivated by the simultaneous demands of data privacy and extreme class imbalance, this study investigated structural failure modes in federated graph intrusion detection, using the FedGATSage pipeline (Al-Tfaily et al., 2025) as a foundational baseline.

An audit of baseline implementations identified two key vulnerabilities: (1) a $46.88\%$ socket-signature leakage rate caused by standard random row partitioning, which artificially elevates reported accuracy; and (2) severe minority-class degradation under standard cross-entropy loss, where $99.83\%$ of rare Backdoor intrusions were misclassified as Injection flows ($0.11\%$ recall). Controlled testing also demonstrated that feature averaging on multi-role IP hosts dilutes subtle attack signatures into dominant background profiles, while Louvain community clustering can generate disconnected community artifacts.

To resolve these structural flaws, we formulated a contrastive hybrid federated architecture validated under an audited, zero-leakage 8-tuple StratifiedGroupKFold protocol across 1,377,837 NetFlow records of the official NF-ToN-IoT benchmark. Our framework integrates three domain-specialized GAT client detectors (Temporal, Content, and Behavioral), well-connected Leiden community partitioning, and a server-side GraphSAGE overlay that reduces communication overhead by $85\%$. To resolve minority collapse, we introduced a joint optimization objective uniting Class-Balanced Focal loss ($\beta = 0.9999, \gamma = 2.0$) with Supervised Contrastive (SupCon) representation regularization ($\mu = 0.1, \tau = 0.07$), supported by dynamic class-balanced sampling ($M = 64$) and intra-class convex edge interpolation.

Across three independent random initialization seeds ($42, 43, 44$), our proposed framework reduced the Macro False Negative Rate from $21.89 \pm 2.01\%$ to $5.72 \pm 1.20\%$ and elevated Balanced Accuracy from $78.11 \pm 2.01\%$ to $94.28 \pm 1.20\%$. Importantly, Supervised Contrastive regularization mitigated the Backdoor detection collapse, reducing Injection misclassifications from 3,430 to 17 flows and raising Backdoor recall from $0.11 \pm 0.08\%$ to $99.57 \pm 0.16\%$. Controlled experiments demonstrated that host-level feature dilution contributes significantly to minority collapse in GNNs, which contrastive metric repulsion helps correct by enforcing geometric class separation.

\section{Future Directions}\label{sec:future_directions}

While the proposed framework achieves outstanding improvements in false-negative reduction and evaluation integrity, the findings of this dissertation illuminate four vital trajectories for future research and technological enhancement:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Class-Specific Contrastive Temperature Scheduling}: In our current formulation, the contrastive temperature parameter $\tau = 0.07$ is fixed uniformly across all attack categories. However, empirical findings on multi-protocol Password attacks reveal that rigid hyperspherical clustering over-penalizes high intra-class variance. Future work should formulate adaptive, class-specific temperature scheduling mechanisms:
    \begin{equation}
    \tau_c = \tau_0 \cdot \left( 1 + \alpha \cdot \text{Var}(\boldsymbol{X}_c) \right)
    \end{equation}
    that dynamically adjust angular compactness based on the natural dispersion of specific attack vectors, preventing recall degradation on complex intrusions.
    \item \textbf{Zero-Day Enterprise Subnet Transfer Learning}: Although the signature-grouped protocol eliminates flow-signature overlap, training and testing sets currently share the underlying IP address space. A crucial frontier lies in developing domain-invariant graph representation architectures capable of generalizing across entirely unseen corporate subnets and enterprise IP topologies without requiring re-training. Integrating domain-adversarial neural networks (DANN) into federated GNN layers could enforce subnet-invariant feature representations.
    \item \textbf{Formal Differential Privacy and Secure Multi-Party Aggregation}: While community centroid pooling compresses edge telemetry by $85\%$ and abstracts raw host IP addresses, advanced membership inference attacks could theoretically reconstruct graph properties from continuous centroid vectors. Future enhancements should incorporate formal $(\epsilon, \delta)$-Differential Privacy guarantees by injecting calibrated Gaussian noise into community centroids before server transmission, coupled with Secure Multi-Party Computation (SMPC) to cryptographically guarantee data confidentiality during FedAvg synchronization.
    \item \textbf{Hardware-in-the-Loop Edge Microcontroller Deployment}: To validate real-world operational feasibility, the proposed client GAT detectors should be compiled and deployed on resource-constrained physical hardware accelerators (such as NVIDIA Jetson Nano edge gateways and Raspberry Pi 4 sensor coordinators). Future research will evaluate INT8 quantization, post-training pruning, and hardware-aware neural architecture search to measure live packet processing throughput (packets per second) and end-to-end inference latency under live network traffic.
\end{enumerate}
